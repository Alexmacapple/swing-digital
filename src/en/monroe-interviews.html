Transcript - Interview - Amy Greene

Milton Greene and Marilyn

What is specific about Milton Greene's photographic work with Marilyn? On a human level as on a business level: they adored each other, they trusted each other. If he had asked her to do a cartwheel, she would have done it. She loved being photographed by him, because the photographs were of a beauty she had never seen before discovering Milton's work.

She had never seen photographs like this, so beautiful, so elegant. She looked at the portfolio and asked: who is this person? The editor of Look, who represented the magazine in Los Angeles, replied: a guy in New York, his name is Milton Greene. She said, well, I want him to photograph me. And Steve replied: perfect, because he wants to photograph you too.

This is how it happened. We both went to California; we were on our honeymoon, we had just gotten married, as incredible as that seems. How did Milton Greene's work differ from that of other photographers? Richard Avedon said it in few words: Milton photographs women like no one else in the world is capable of, including me.

He had a special relationship with women. And then he was an artist, a true artist, with an extraordinary spirit. What made Milton Greene get into production? And why did he take all these risks? He had reached that level, but he wanted to go even higher.

He loved cinema, of course, he had always loved films, he had grown up in a world of fiction, and he wanted to take the next step. She was enthusiastic about the idea of this company being created around her. You meet someone, you like them, you get to know them, and trust builds.

This was Milton's strength with all the people he photographed: whoever he photographed knew he would never hurt them. The word is trust. In 1954, Marilyn left Fox and came to your home in Weston, Connecticut. What memories do you have from this period? Do you have an anecdote to tell us? She would never have hurt me in any way.

Weston, Connecticut

So I knew there was nothing between them. A lot of people say I'm crazy, and that... She was fine. She was a young woman who was a sponge: she absorbed everything, from everywhere. During Milton's time running Marilyn Monroe Productions, you and Marilyn spent a lot of time together. What united you then? We became friends.

I didn't need to call her to ask her to go to the Met: she was in the room next to mine. It was a family atmosphere, and she loved it. Joshua was there, he was two years old; she loved him and he loved her. It was a shared happiness. I hate that it sounds so cheesy, but it's true: she's never given me any trouble.

First of all, I don't think she would have dared. And then everyone came to our house, to the countryside. Every Sunday evening, there were fifteen or sixteen of us around the table. Marilyn was there, she participated in the conversation and she held her place. I have a funny story to tell you.

One Sunday evening — Milton had built this table himself, he thought he was a carpenter — everyone was sitting around, and a guest said he was coming back from a funeral. We started talking about death. I said: very good, let's go around the table and let everyone tell us what they think. And it went from one to the other. Exciting people talking.

The turn has come to Marilyn. She said: I just want to be buried under a headstone that says 36, 22, 36. Do you know what that was? His measurements. That's why she had this wonderful sense of humor. She received a standing ovation. There are beautiful photographs by Milton Greene where we see Marilyn with your son Joshua. How did Marilyn view him?

Marilyn Monroe remains a global icon, and many people have adopted her image. Was this phenomenon predictable at the time of Marilyn Monroe Productions? It was The Prince and the Showgirl, and she had never been happier than at that moment in her life. Moreover, Joshua Logan, the great director, the one whose first name Joshua Greene bears, said that it was his golden period.

He was the one who directed her in Bus Stop. Another very important thing: Marilyn Monroe Productions gave her the right to choose her director, something she had never had before. Again, it was Lew Wasserman, Milton Greene, Jay Kanter. All this was negotiated over the phone with Zanuck. Zanuck never understood her. Zanuck never liked her. He called him strawhead. But it doesn't matter.

The Marilyn Monroe Productions

How do you explain that Marilyn became an icon? This generation — no, not this one, the last two — seems to no longer have heroes like we had. So they need to make one. And Marilyn was there. She was dead before this happened. She would have been as stunned as the rest of us. Nobody understood this phenomenon.

Besides, one of our terrible friends, who thought he was a comedian, said when Marilyn died that it was a good career plan. And it was. If she had lived, maybe none of this would have happened. But she also surpasses James Dean. She surpasses Steve McQueen. It's at a level we've never seen before. Maybe a king or a queen.

Perhaps Queen Victoria, in the Victorian era. My son Joshua always says she died young. She died at thirty-six years old. And he's right. It's still this image of youth: Dean, Elvis, Jimi Hendrix, Janis Joplin. If we want to bring them all together: Mozart, thirty-six years old. Modigliani, thirty-six years old.

There's a reason these people become icons, and I think it's on to something: they died young. No one could see their faults. Arthur was jealous of Milton. Not about Milton as a man, but about the time Marilyn and Milton spent working together. Arthur couldn't stand it. It was beyond his strength.

He was possessive, and he felt it was his time. Well, they were making a film, all the same. Let's be charitable: Arthur has three children, and I don't mean to be mean. Let's just say he was no match. He couldn't handle what Kanter, Greene and Wasserman were handling. He didn't understand. It wasn't his job, it wasn't his means.

If he had gone and sat in a corner to write, he wouldn't have bothered anyone. We prayed for that: God, let Arthur go and write a play. But no. He came to the set. He came into the dressing room. He was picking her up, and she didn't want him. She really didn't want it, because she was working.

Becoming an icon

When Laurence Olivier is directing you, you have to be attentive, even when you don't feel like it. And Arthur had become impossible. This is what got the better of Marilyn Monroe Productions. I had an unhappy husband. I had a husband who didn't really want to do what he did. We lived very well, we lived magnificently from his talent, we were accustomed to a very pleasant life.

And suddenly there was an unhappy man at home. Years later, during the filming of The Misfits, my friend Eli Wallach, whom I adored — a very well-known actor in New York — was on the set: he played one of the characters. At one point, Marilyn turned against Arthur. They argued constantly on set.

She turned against him, she started shouting, reproaching him for this and that. And she said, among other things: you even took from me the only person I trusted in my entire life. You took it away from me. Arthur replied: What are you talking about? Marilyn said: by Milton Greene. Why isn't he there? Why isn't he on this set? It was Eli who told me about it.

You have your answer. Wait, let's start from the beginning. I dreamed of Marilyn, and she was calling for help. I wake up my husband and tell him: take a plane and go see her. He replied: you've lost your mind, we're going to Paris in three days for the collections, what do you want? It's impossible, I'm packing my bags, I'm leaving. I said: very good.

If you don't want to go see her, call her. So he called Arthur Jacobs, who was the press officer, and Arthur immediately gave him the number. They spoke for three hours. Not an hour: three hours that day.

Then she called him back the next day, they spoke again and they set up a meeting: at the end of the collections, he would take the plane by the Pole and go to Los Angeles, and I would return to New York. It was agreed. And you know what happened. Do you know what was said during these conversations? No, my little one. I left the room, it was none of my business.

I was sure of myself at this point. Exactly. No. They were partners. Likewise, I didn't listen: I left the room when he was talking to announcers. I'm not that kind of woman, never have been. I feel very good like that. So she was in a cocoon, and the butterfly was coming out of the cocoon, along with all those around it.

And she loved it. She had no problems except Arthur. Well, okay, I'm not going to talk about Arthur: the day is too beautiful. Arthur could never understand the relationship between Milton and Marilyn. Never in his life could he have understood her. And Marilyn Monroe Productions was fifty-one percent Marilyn.

It was Milton who did this, along with Lew Wasserman and Jay Kanter, so that she would have the majority of her own production company. Arthur did not understand why Milton Greene owned forty-nine percent of Marilyn Monroe Productions, because he never knew what Milton did on a daily basis.

One day, Gene Kelly came to the house and he said: I came back from Paris, I saw a play in French — Gene spoke French — Irma la Douce. I optioned it, and we're going to take it to Broadway. Milton said: absolutely. Marilyn was taken into confidence that same evening, and was told that she was going to play Irma la Douce on Broadway.

What is Sweet Irma? She loved it. A prostitute, this, that, in love with a policeman, and music. She was delighted. This is the protection that Milton Greene gave him, and which Arthur never understood. It was daily, hourly, constant protection. How was she a modern woman? She became a feminist.

She suddenly started asking questions and demanding answers, from Zanuck and from everyone she spoke to. She loved Carl Sandburg, she went to meet him in Chicago.

A modern woman

One very important thing: The first time Marilyn picked up the phone to tell someone "I demand that you do this" was when she learned that her favorite singer, Ella Fitzgerald, was scheduled to perform at the Crescendo and that they didn't want her, because she was African-American. Marilyn picked up the phone and said, you have to let her come.

And if you do, I'll be there every night. She was there every evening. And she and Ella became friends. It was there that she first became aware of her power. One evening we went to see Sinatra at the Copacabana. The room was packed, you couldn't have fit a toothpick in there.

We were coming out of a big press evening: she wore a white satin dress, white satin shoes, a white mink coat, shaved mink, diamonds. She looked like a movie star. And I tell him: don't count on it, we'll never get in. They know Milton, and if they told him no on the phone, we won't come in. She asked me: how badly do you want to come in?

I replied: I'm in love with this man, so of course I want to come in and see him. She said: okay, follow me. We leave the party, we enter the Copacabana — not through the kitchens, as usual: if you remember the Goodfellas scene, that's how we entered. But this time we went down the stairs.

There was a small landing before the last three steps. She stopped there, without saying a word. Me behind her, Milton behind me. She stayed there. And everyone turned in that direction. Frank was on the other side, and he realizes that something is happening, because no one is paying attention to him anymore. He asks: what? Who is it ? What is happening?

He stops the show. I was very impressed. I said, okay, I owe you one. A mistake. Just a mistake. Milton and I... We've been asked this question forever. She simply took the two extra pills, the ones she should never have taken, because she had forgotten that she had already taken them three hours earlier. It was a mistake. The Cubans were not after her.

The gangsters weren't after her. The Kennedys weren't after her. It was a mistake, period. Only, an error, there is nothing romantic about it. So of course, no one wants to believe it. She was wrong. It's that simple. She was wrong. It was not premeditated. Because she had those three conversations with Milton.

What she left

She died while we were in Paris, for the collections. He thought she was happy, he thought she was safe, and they couldn't wait to see each other again. If she hadn't died, I'm sure Marilyn Monroe Productions would have left again. But that never happened. A mistake. Sexual freedom: I believe that this is its real contribution.

I don't think she knew what she was doing while she was doing it, but this sexual freedom that is everywhere today — except in the Middle East, but let's not get into that. Be happy with your body. Love your body. Take care of your body. Be sexy. Everything we see today, which is excessive, is already too much: there is never a happy medium, it is always too much or too little.

So I believe that what she brought to the world in general was sexual freedom. Blonde hair: more women have dyed their hair blonde than even in Jean Harlow's era. They hadn't done it as much as with Marilyn. Let's not forget that Betty Grable was blonde. We have always liked blondes.

Jean Harlow was beautiful, everything about her was beautiful: if you watch a Jean Harlow movie, look at her feet, her ankles, her legs. They are magnificent. Betty Grable, same. Every woman has something to give to cinema. It's their job, and it's what they have to do, because that's why they get paid a lot of money. So there was Harlow, then Grable, then Marilyn.

Dietrich was in a world of her own: Dietrich was never said to be a blonde, if you know what I mean. She was blonde, but she was something else entirely. Who else comes to mind? Eva Marie Saint, in the sixties. But then again, she wasn't sensual enough for that.

So I think what she left is that you have the right to dye your hair and be blonde, if that's what you want. She gave women their freedom. Women are great, but we don't know it. We haven't understood what we are worth, really. We can do anything.

Writing a Blonde in Manhattan

What made you want to write A Blonde in Manhattan? I was looking for a book topic. I wrote a book called The Man from Tiananmen Square, which was a book about this man who stopped a column of tanks in 1989, in Tiananmen Square, in Beijing.

I wanted to continue this work on photography, since the book was essentially about these photographers who had captured this moment and how the photo had evolved over time to become an icon, a poster, diverted in a thousand ways.

A few years before, I had purchased a Marilyn Monroe photo book, which was a set of photos taken in 1955 during Marilyn's stay in New York. Curiously, in this book, there was a text which spoke a lot about Marilyn Monroe and which said perhaps three lines no more about the author of the photos, the photographer Ed Feingirch.

I understood that there was an interesting subject because the photos were very well known, there was in particular in this set the photo of Marilyn putting on perfume with a bottle of Chanel number 5. I was thinking who could be this man who took such well-known photos and in the photo album itself, there are not three lines about him.

My idea was to confront this extremely photographed woman, this extremely well-known silhouette, this anonymous photographer. That's how I started looking into who the man behind the photos was. There were two reverse books, ultimately. On the one hand, a very famous man who stops a column of tanks and whom everyone knows but whose identity no one knows.

And on the other, an extremely well-known subject, but here, it is the photographer who is unknown. Have you encountered any difficulties? A lot has been written about Marilyn Monroe, there have been a lot of books. That is to say, overwhelmed by the weight of the myth and in relation to all the ideas received or not that we may have?

Not really because for me, it wasn't just a book about Marilyn Monroe. That is to say, Marilyn Monroe is a character that interested me a lot, but what interested me was this meeting between Ed Feingers and Marilyn Monroe. And the difficulty was rather finding information on this photographer. Marilyn, there were a lot of things on hand.

It was rather relaxing for me, in front of this completely unknown figure of Ed Feingers, to have in front of me a lot of documents on Marilyn. The difficulty was to sort things out and not let myself be absorbed solely by Marilyn and not make a book that was about Marilyn or that copied other biographies of Marilyn that I had read elsewhere. So no, it's more at the exit.

It's very curious, when we release a book on Marilyn, to become, basically, an element in an enormous machine and to see that there will necessarily be 15 books afterwards, that there will be 850 before, and that we are only a small point in an enormous library devoted to Marilyn Monroe. That's very, very strange. This has never happened to me on any other book.

We have the impression, when we read the book, that you are telling us a big story, through a small story, through portraits of people, through humanities, is that what interests you, ultimately? This is the story of a week. But this week, she actually says a lot of things.

I am quite convinced that a good way to perceive people is to focus on limited moments in time or on a single aspect of their personality.

That is to say, I don't really believe in these biographies, especially the American biographers, which are enormous, which are full of details and where we learn the number of bars that the bed of the kid who would later become a great artist had. And we make paving stones like that, which is, as they say, the definitive biography. For me, it's impossible to do. People's lives are too complex.

On the other hand, if we manage to isolate, whether it's five minutes in the story of the local man or a week in the life of Marilyn, I think we can learn a lot of things through that. Well, that's what I believe. And this time spent with them, finally, at the end of the book, after all this time, with their ass, what did it mean to you?

I think it started out as a book about Ed Feynger and Marilyn Monroe, but beyond that, it's a book that talks about time, about the way people fit into their times. And this is what I realized only afterwards, and in particular the evolution of techniques. That is, what killed Ed Feynger?

It's the fact that he clung to a world that had collapsed and that he continued to take photos as in 1955, as we entered the 60s, that he refused to do color, that he did not adapt to the new format of magazines and that he continued to do something which, in any case, no longer existed.

Of course, we know what she died of, addictions, etc. But for me, he is someone who remained frozen in a cinema, which is this great Hollywood cinema, which ceased to exist little by little after his death. That is to say, it is impossible to see Marilyn other than in this great, magnificent Hollywood cinema.

We cannot project it in the cinema which will be the cinema of the 60s and even less of the 70s. So if we want to survive, we must adapt to the times. You can't be stronger than time, even Marilyn Monroe, even Eddie Feingart. And both of these characters died physically because they refused to move on, to move on to the next step.

In your story, you really choose a particular moment in Marilyn's life. Can you tell us about this moment? Yes, so it's the year 1955 and it's a breakout year for her, since she's very well known. She is under contract with a major studio.

She could have a career as an actress under contract and she chooses to break this contract illegally to go and found her own production company in New York. And it's something... Of course, there were actors who founded their production companies at the time. Everyone tries to do that, from John Wayne to Marlon Brando, but she's a woman.

And she's not an actress who's known for having the charisma to do something like that. What she does is a very violent move to take a plane illegally and move to New York to set up a production company and become not only an actress, but also in film production. For her, it's perhaps the happiest year of her life, I think, the year 55.

That is to say that Marilyn Monroe was often said to be late for her shoots, etc. It's true, but it's someone who knows photography. Maybe instinctively, maybe not theoretically. When you see the way she poses, the way she plays with the camera, she is perhaps the greatest model there has ever been, beyond her acting career. When we see what she has left to the history of photography, it is considerable, there is not one photo similar to another.

And so I think she knows how to recognize, by the way Fenger moves around her, the way he positions himself, that she is facing someone very gifted. And so there is recognition.

And himself, I think he also sees that he is facing an extraordinary model, that he will bring him something new and that he will add something new to his career as a war photographer and reporting photographer.

So in my opinion, there is a mutual respect which is contractual, that is to say what is fascinating in the last photos of the report where we see Marilyn on an elephant during a charity gala, is that Fenger returned to what he was with the photos of the skirt, that is to say a photographer among others.

But nevertheless, for a week, and that's why she agrees to follow him to places where she doesn't usually go, there is a mutual respect between two very great professionals. And then, moreover, but they didn't have the time in a week to discuss this, they are linked simply by their era, that is to say that they are both representative of their era, not of the same aspect of their era. And he, from the beginning of photojournalism, with his small cameras and with the reality that we can confront with these new techniques and these field photographers who really emerge in these post-war years, during the war and in the post-war period. That is to say, if he were a filmmaker, he would be John Cassavetes, he would be someone of improvisation, who comes out of the studios, who is in the street, he embodies a certain form of freedom that she came to seek in New York, him as a photographer, because he is that type of photographer.

And these moments of spontaneity are also those of Marilyn Monroe, that is to say the most extraordinary photos of Marilyn Monroe, that is to say that really, she is there, in front of us, alive, and that's what makes her fascinating, but that's what's exhausting.

You can't live long by only living in the present, it's impossible, and these two had chosen to live like that, and inevitably, it meant a very short life. I'm not sure that by taking all those sleeping pills and drinking all that alcohol, I was aware in 1955 that it was a path to death. It was a path to excess, for sure, but we weren't there yet. I know that in Hollywood at the time, there were even doctors who prescribed LSD.

We know that Gary Grant was a user of LSD, but completely legal. That is to say, I think that this is a time where science is seen as something necessarily beneficial and therefore drugs cannot kill. So of course, when you look at Ed Feingarsch's drinking and his drug use, Marilyn Monroe's crazy drug use, they were definitely going to kill themselves.

It's obvious, but seen from today, I'm not sure that people were aware at the time, when they took all this, that it was the sure path to downfall. There is something of the desire to erase yourself from the world when you take on so much. We are in a particular period, we are in the cinema of the 50s. How could you describe to us this cinema of Marilyn, the cinema of this era? It’s studio cinema first and foremost.

We are at the end of the golden age of studios, that is to say where the stars are really under contract with a studio which is very powerful and which has absolute power over the lives of the stars. This will be the birth of the power of stars and in particular of agents. And we're not quite there yet. Marilyn is trying this crazy move of creating her production company, but we are still at a moment where the stars are producers, big studio bosses.

For me, Elisabeth Taylor is an actress that I'm less familiar with, but she's Cleopatra, that is to say she has this calm side, while Marilyn is really a very great musical comedy actress. There is something quite modern in Marilyn's voice. Marilyn is a dancer. And the real rebel, deep down, is still Marilyn. And how would you characterize Marilyn’s voice for you?

What does it mean? It's a voice that is very worked to begin with. She was able to have an acting voice. That is to say something very calm, very calm, very sweet, very sensual too. But it is a voice that also evolves as the films progress.

I believe that the voice of Marilyn in the Misfits is not quite the voice of Marilyn in some Lamb Show or in this year of reflection. So it's definitely an actress' voice. Just like his body was worked on, like his haircut. I think she managed to find a very special and unique voice.

It is very recognizable, the voice of Marilyn Monroe, as much as her face. And why do you think it became a myth? First of all, I think she's a great actress. It's something we don't say enough.

But there is something very, very strong when we see Marilyn on screen what happens, then she had a short life and at the same time filled with twists and turns, extremely interesting things. She lived through an era that still fascinates today. She took a lot of photos. We are still discovering photos today that we did not know of this woman.

So she offered herself to the photographic lens that very, very few actresses have done, maybe even none. She embodies a completely original and new heroism. And then, through her death, which some try to explain endlessly, she remains alive, that is to say that there are always new theories which are developed depending on the times.
