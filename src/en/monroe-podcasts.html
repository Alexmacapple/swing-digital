  5. Transcript - Podcast - The Archives

    Milton Greene

    Milton Greene is a photographer. For years, millions of us have seen his images on the covers of Look, Life, Vogue and other magazines. Thirty-three-year-old Milton, his wife, and their one-year-old son live in this one-hundred-and-fifty-year-old house in Weston, Connecticut. It's about an hour's drive from his studio in Manhattan. Milton Greene was my mentor.

    He was my best friend: growing up around him, learning photography with him, learning to shoot images with him. When I decided to put away my devices and enter the restaurant business, it was out of love for my father. He died heartbroken that some of the best work he ever did was never published, never seen, and could never be reproduced.

    When I discovered digital, I saw the opportunity to bring my father back to me, and to bring photos back to the world. I think it's important to capture these images as well as the technology allows. Technology continues to change, so sometimes we start over and over again. There are only a limited number of photographs: four thousand in the entire collaboration between Milton Greene and Marilyn Monroe.

    Restore Images

    Let's work on all the ones we can. What we do is go into the image and capture the eye, the lips, making sure we retain detail in the highlights and in the shadows. We therefore export from FlexColor, the software supplied with the scanner, to Photoshop. From start to finish, this averages forty to sixty hours, depending on the image.

    Most of the cleaning is done pixel by pixel with the correction tool. This can take thirty to forty hours of work. What people don't really measure is the level of detail of this work; when you really manipulate these pixels, you have to be careful not to create a digital scar. Here we see the state of the film.

    This is dust embedded on the surface of the film. A scratch here, some scratches there. These are small things, but see how it interferes with the hair. It's all there. Rav drew a clipping, then softened it according to the softness of the film. Once I have the mask, it allows me to choose which color correction layer tools I'm going to use.

    Masks and prints

    Most of the time, it will be a choice between hue and saturation, curves and selective correction. Sometimes there will be two or three adjustment layers with a mask. Other times there can be as many as six. On the face and hair, getting the right colors requires more control with masks.

    We'll probably print seven proofs to feel how the image prints and how it looks on screen, because the screen gives a lot more information than you can really get on paper. But I need to see the prints enlarged to really see the edges of the masks, how they merge, and if there is an error that needs to be corrected.

    When I was eleven, my father put me in the darkroom and taught me how to shoot. His way of approaching the image, what he wanted to strengthen, darken, retain or control, made it possible to get the best out of the image. The print therefore ended up looking different from the original completed piece of film. That's what a fine art printer does: they bring another layer of expertise so that the print holds up on its own.

    The original Look magazine commission that led Milton to photograph Marilyn was actually to photograph several promising young talents in Hollywood. Look editors presented Milton's portfolio to Marilyn. When she saw his photographs, she was very excited to work with him.

    Marilyn in front of the camera

    They met in California and did the mandolin and negligee session on the first day. Watching the films the next morning, Marilyn saw in Milton's photograph a part of herself that was real. He would never have let anyone see a bad photo if he had taken one, and she could believe he was protective.

    This is how their relationship as photographer and muse was built. In the months that followed, Milton returned to California to photograph Marilyn. They would go to the costume department at 20th Century Fox, put on a fancy outfit, then go off to shoot on the outdoor sets. Over a short period, we see photographs of Marilyn playing dress-up, whether she is a peasant, a gypsy or a prostitute.

    There is an element of fantasy and pleasure in their collaboration. They were having fun. It was make-believe. All Milton wanted to do was prove that she could be the composing actress she wanted to be, and build her confidence.
